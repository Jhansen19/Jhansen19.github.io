    <div class="section-label">Who I am</div>
    <h2>A builder with a data science toolkit and an operations backbone</h2>
    <p>
      I am a builder at heart. When I see a messy problem, my instinct is to understand it, shape it, and make something useful from it. That pattern has followed me through fifteen years of operations leadership, supply chain planning, product work, business building, and now hands on data science.
    </p>
    <p>
      My path into data science was not a sharp career turn. It was a formal deepening of work I had already been doing. I had used data to justify a major manufacturing investment, redesign warehouse flows, find new labor markets, improve production visibility, and make better planning decisions. The M.S. in Data Science from Indiana University gave me the technical depth to take that practical problem solving further.
    </p>
    <p>
      What drives me is the moment when scattered facts become a clear decision. I like work that makes teams sharper, makes systems easier to run, and gives people enough clarity to act with confidence.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section class="section-card purpose-band">
    <div class="section-label">What drives me</div>
    <h2>Building a world that cares for people</h2>
    <p>
      The most important question in any role is whether the work makes things genuinely better for the people it touches. Not just the business. The people inside the operation, the communities connected to it, and the generation of children growing up alongside the systems we are building right now. I want to contribute to a world with more social cohesion, where people help each other develop, support each other when things are hard, and build together rather than taking advantage of each other.
    </p>
    <p>
      That belief shapes how I approach data science and supply chain work. Good logistics networks should reduce waste and extend access, not just cut cost. Good data tools should help people make honest decisions, not just validate what leadership already wanted to hear. Good automation should make work more humane, not just more efficient. I moved to Copenhagen with my family in 2025, partly because Denmark's way of thinking about shared wellbeing and investing in people reflects how I try to approach the work I do.
    </p>

  <section class="section-card">
    <div class="section-label">How I lead</div>
    <h2>Clearing the path and doing the work</h2>
    <p>
      I do not treat leadership and contribution as separate roles. In practice they happen in the same week, often the same day. The mode shifts depending on what the team and the moment require.
    </p>
    <div class="leadership-split">
      <div class="leadership-mode leadership-mode--lead">
        <h3>Clearing the path for others</h3>
        <p>When I joined Inform as Operations Manager in Skopje, the company was hiring quickly and IT was falling behind. Every new person arrived at an IT team with no time to properly set them up. New hires were sitting idle for days after starting.</p>
        <p>It looked like an IT capacity problem. It was not. I sat with both sides and found that the hiring team and IT had never fully mapped the handoff between them. Each group was doing its part, but the connection between them was broken. I built a new process so IT had what it needed before each hire arrived. Onboarding time dropped from weeks to days.</p>
        <p>Most blocked work looks like a resource problem from the outside. Inside it is usually a communication or process gap waiting for someone to trace it back to its source. That is the work I do as a leader.</p>
        <ul>
          <li>Identify where friction actually lives, not just where it shows up</li>
          <li>Build bridges between teams who cannot see each other's constraints</li>
          <li>Establish steady rhythms so problems surface early</li>
          <li>Make decisions when they are needed so others can move forward</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="leadership-mode leadership-mode--member">
        <h3>Building alongside the team</h3>
        <p>When I was appointed to build MVP Landscaping, I was the first and only employee. There was no team, no processes, no brand. MVP was a social enterprise built to provide real employment to people re-entering the workforce after difficult circumstances. That mission shaped how I built from day one.</p>
        <p>I grew the crew to six people over two years. I was not recruiting people to receive instructions. I was bringing people into something we were building together. The standard procedures were written alongside the people who would use them. I was on the equipment with the crew, not managing from above. MVP reached roughly half a million dollars in annual revenue because the team had genuine ownership of the work.</p>
        <p>That instinct has carried through every team I have built or joined since. Buy-in does not come from being told what to do. It comes from helping shape what you are doing.</p>
        <ul>
          <li>Write the code, map the process, take the meeting alongside the team</li>
          <li>Co-create standards with the people who will use them</li>
          <li>Bring a business perspective into technical conversations</li>
          <li>Stay close enough to the work to know when something is going wrong</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
